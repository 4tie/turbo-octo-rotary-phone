## Completed Interview Responses

### 1. How I actually made a real decision

The most significant decision I made in the last year was not immediately returning to the same kind of job after losing mine. My first instinct was to find another similar job because it was familiar and offered short-term stability, especially with financial pressure and responsibility toward my parents.

I gathered information mainly through action rather than a formal planning process. I researched and experimented with AI models, coding agents, automation, Freqtrade, backtesting, trading platforms, APIs, and possible software business models. I built projects, tested tools, encountered failures, and gradually learned what was realistic.

The decision took time because I kept moving mentally between two options: returning to the safer path or using the period to build skills, systems, and something I could own. The actual moment of deciding did not feel like confidence. It felt like accepting uncertainty and choosing the risk that might give me more long-term control.

### 2. Fast and iterative vs. deliberate upfront

I am generally more comfortable moving fast, testing something real, and correcting course based on evidence. I learn better through building and experimentation than through trying to predict every problem in advance.

This changes with the stakes. When failure is cheap, contained, and reversible — such as research, prototypes, model tests, workflow experiments, and backtests — I prefer speed. When real money, family security, important data, production systems, or irreversible consequences are involved, I slow down and want stronger evidence, clear limits, and a recovery plan.

The rule is: move quickly when failure is recoverable and informative; slow down when failure could create meaningful financial, technical, personal, or reputational damage.

### 3. My actual risk tolerance

My actual risk tolerance is moderate, but my emotional tolerance is lower than my decisions may suggest. I can deliberately take meaningful risks, but I carry them mentally, monitor them closely, and do not relax easily when the downside feels unclear or outside my control.

With money, the threshold depends on what the money represents. I can tolerate losing a limited amount deliberately set aside for a recoverable experiment. I become uncomfortable when failure could affect living expenses, support for my parents, or my financial safety buffer. My working test is: “If this fails completely, will I still be financially stable?” If the answer is uncertain, the risk is too high.

I have a higher tolerance for technical failure when it happens safely. Broken prototypes, failed tests, and weak strategies are useful information. My tolerance falls sharply when a technical action could delete important data, expose credentials, damage a working production system, or affect real users without a rollback path.

For reputation, I am comfortable being seen learning, trying difficult things, or releasing an imperfect early version. I am not comfortable being dishonest, exaggerating evidence, or claiming an unproven trading or AI system is safe, reliable, or profitable.

### 4. How I want options presented

I want a clear recommendation first rather than a neutral list that leaves all the judgment to me.

The preferred structure is:

1. Recommendation — what I should choose.
2. Why — the reasoning and supporting evidence.
3. Main downside — the most important risk or weakness.
4. Best alternative — the strongest other option and when it would be better.
5. Next action — what should happen now.

If the evidence is weak or the options are genuinely close, say that clearly and identify the smallest safe test that would help decide. Recommendations should reflect my actual priorities: financial stability, limited downside, independence, reliability, and avoiding unnecessary complexity.

### 5. Reversible vs. irreversible decisions

I move quickly on reversible decisions because I can test the choice, observe the result, and change direction without serious damage. Examples include trying a different AI model, adjusting a prototype, changing a workflow, or running a new backtest.

I slow down significantly on irreversible or difficult-to-reverse decisions. Examples include placing a meaningful amount of real money into live trading, deleting important data, deploying a major architectural change, giving an AI control over funds or accounts, or signing an agreement that limits future options.

The difference is the cost of being wrong. Before a high-consequence action, I want the maximum acceptable loss defined, the assumptions checked, the downside understood, and a rollback or recovery plan established wherever possible.
