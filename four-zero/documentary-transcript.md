# Documentary Transcript — Day 6, Zero One Systems Curriculum

---

**Q1: Where did you start? Not professionally — I mean at the beginning. What was your situation, your background, what was the world you grew up in, and how did it shape how you think about work and money?**

A: I started in a financially difficult environment, with limited opportunities, family pressure, and no clear career direction. Money was not something I could treat casually, because financial pressure affected real decisions and responsibilities around me.

That shaped the way I think about work. I tend to look at work as something that should create security, independence, and practical results — not just status or a job title. I became interested in technology, AI, automation, and trading because they seemed like paths where skills and systems could create opportunities that were not otherwise available to me.

It also made me careful with money, but at the same time very motivated to find ways to improve my situation. I am drawn to projects that could become useful products, generate income, or reduce dependence on unstable work. This is one reason I care so much about building systems properly and proving that they actually work.

Because I did not begin with a clear roadmap, much of my learning has been self-directed. I often learn by building, testing, making mistakes, and improving things step by step. That has made me analytical and persistent, but it can also make me focus heavily on finding the right path and avoiding wasted time or money.

Overall, my background taught me that work should lead somewhere meaningful. I value independence, reliability, practical skills, and opportunities that can create long-term financial stability for me and the people I am responsible for.

---

**Follow-up: Who are the people you're responsible for, and how much does that responsibility shape the decisions you make about your work?**

A: The people I feel most responsible for are my parents. A major part of what drives me is wanting to support them and create greater financial security for my family.

That responsibility strongly shapes the decisions I make about work. I am not only thinking about personal interest or career status; I am thinking about stability, dependable income, and building something that can improve our situation over the long term.

It is one reason I am drawn to technology, AI, automation, and trading-related systems. I see them as possible paths toward independence and opportunities that may not be available through a traditional career alone.

At the same time, this responsibility makes uncertainty feel heavier. I want to take meaningful opportunities, but I also need to avoid wasting money, making reckless decisions, or depending on projects that have not been properly tested.

Ultimately, I want my work to give me four things: financial stability, the ability to support my parents, greater independence, and less uncertainty about the future.

---

**Q2: Tell me about a decision that changed your trajectory. Not the one you'd tell someone in a job interview — the real one. What were you thinking? What did you nearly do instead?**

A: The decision that changed my direction came when my job ended and I had to decide whether to stay on the same path or take a major risk and build something different.

At the time, I was thinking mainly about security. Staying in the same kind of work would have been the safer and more familiar option. It would have meant less uncertainty in the short term, and part of me wanted to choose that because I had financial pressure and responsibilities toward my parents.

But I also knew that returning to the same path would probably lead me back to the same position: limited growth, limited control, and continued uncertainty about the future. I did not want to spend years depending entirely on work that could disappear again.

So I chose to take the risk of focusing more seriously on technology, AI, software, automation, and trading systems. I was not completely confident, and I did not have a perfect plan. I was trying to create a path where my results could depend more on the skills and systems I built than on whether someone continued employing me.

What I nearly did instead was look for another similar job immediately and treat everything else as a side interest. That would have felt safer, but I believed it might delay the larger change I needed to make.

The decision was not simply about changing careers. It was about choosing uncertainty with the possibility of independence over familiarity with limited control. It increased the pressure on me, but it also changed how seriously I approached learning, building, and creating something that could eventually support both me and my family.

---

**Q3: Tell me about a failure. One that actually cost you something — time, money, a relationship, a version of yourself you'd been holding onto. What happened?**

A: The failure that cost me the most was not one single event. It was the combination of losing money through trading and losing a large amount of time on projects that never became what I hoped they would.

With trading, I was trying to find a faster way to improve my financial situation. Because I already felt pressure around money and responsibility toward my parents, the idea of making income through trading felt important. But I entered before I had enough experience, a proven system, or strong risk controls. I focused too much on the possible return and not enough on how quickly losses could happen.

The money I lost mattered, but the bigger cost was what it did to my confidence. It forced me to admit that motivation, intelligence, and effort are not enough when the process is weak. A strategy that looks promising is not necessarily profitable, and a few successful trades do not prove that someone knows what they are doing.

At the same time, I spent a lot of time building software and AI projects that became too complicated, changed direction repeatedly, or never reached a truly usable final product. I would become excited about the potential, add more features, redesign the plan, or move to a different tool before fully completing and validating what already existed.

That cost me months of effort and created the feeling that I was always working but not always moving forward. I was holding onto the idea that one big project or one successful strategy might suddenly solve everything. Letting go of that idea was difficult.

What I learned from both failures is that I need evidence before confidence. In trading, that means realistic backtesting, fees, slippage, out-of-sample testing, strict risk limits, and accepting that some strategies should be rejected. In software, it means finishing smaller working systems, testing the real user flow, avoiding unnecessary complexity, and not confusing constant activity with progress.

These failures made me more careful, but they also clarified what I am trying to build. I no longer want to depend on hope, shortcuts, or one lucky result. I want systems that can prove themselves before I trust them with my time, money, or future.

---

**Q4: What's your actual relationship with money and risk? Not what you think it should be — what is it, honestly? Do you sleep well when things are uncertain?**

A: My relationship with money and risk is complicated. I want security badly, but I am also willing to take risks because I do not believe the safest path will necessarily get me where I need to go.

Money represents more than comfort to me. It means independence, the ability to support my parents, and relief from uncertainty. Because I grew up with financial difficulty and later lost my job, not having enough money feels like a real threat rather than an abstract concern.

That pressure sometimes pushes me in opposite directions. Part of me wants to protect every resource I have and avoid losing anything. Another part feels that I need to take a significant risk to change my situation. This has led me toward trading, technology, AI, and ambitious projects that could create opportunities, but it has also sometimes made me expect too much from them too quickly.

Honestly, I do not sleep particularly well when things are uncertain — especially when the uncertainty involves money, unfinished work, or whether I will be able to support my family. I tend to keep thinking about the problem, searching for solutions, changing plans, or trying to regain a sense of control.

I am capable of taking risk, but I am not naturally relaxed about it. I often carry the risk mentally even after I have chosen it. I can become overly focused on finding the right system or project that will finally create stability, which sometimes makes it difficult to slow down or accept gradual progress.

My biggest weakness is that financial pressure can make a risky opportunity feel more necessary — and therefore more reasonable — than it truly is. My past trading losses and unfinished projects showed me that urgency can distort judgment.

What I am trying to develop now is controlled risk: testing before trusting, limiting what I can lose, finishing smaller steps, and separating a promising idea from something that has actually been proven. I still want meaningful upside, but I do not want hope or financial pressure to make decisions for me.

---

**Q5: What drives you when no one's watching? Not the public answer — the real one.**

A: What drives me when no one is watching is a mix of pressure, fear, responsibility, and the belief that I can still change my situation.

A large part of it is financial. I do not want to stay dependent on unstable work, limited opportunities, or decisions made by other people. I want enough control over my life that losing a job or facing another setback does not put everything at risk.

My parents are also a major part of that drive. I want to reach a point where I can support them properly and reduce the pressure on my family. That responsibility makes it difficult for me to treat my work casually.

There is also something personal underneath it. I want to prove to myself that the time I have spent learning, building, failing, and starting again was not wasted. I do not want to remain someone who always has ideas but never turns them into something real.

Sometimes what drives me is fear: fear of wasting more time, staying stuck, choosing the wrong path, or failing to create stability. That fear can be useful because it keeps me moving, but it can also make me impatient and push me toward doing too many things at once.

At my best, I am driven by the desire to build something real, useful, and independent — something that works without pretending, produces genuine value, and gives me and my family more security.

When no one is watching, I am not chasing recognition. I am trying to create a life where I feel less trapped, less uncertain, and more capable of taking care of the people who matter to me.

---

**Q6: What do you want your work to mean? If it all goes right, what does that look like — and how does that compare to what you thought it would look like five years ago?**

A: I want my work to mean that I managed to create stability and independence from a difficult starting point.

If everything goes right, I am not simply earning more money. I have built something real — probably through AI, software, automation, or trading systems — that creates consistent value and does not depend entirely on one employer or one source of income.

I want my work to give me enough financial stability to support my parents properly, reduce uncertainty for my family, and stop feeling that one setback could undo everything. I want to be able to make decisions based on what is right, not only on what I can afford to risk losing.

I also want the systems I build to be genuinely useful. I do not want to create another project that looks impressive but does not work in reality. Whether it is an AI that researches and validates profitable Freqtrade strategies, an agent that helps manage trading safely, or another software product, I want it to solve a real problem and prove its value.

Success would also mean becoming someone who finishes. Someone who can take a complicated idea, reduce it to a practical system, test it properly, and turn it into something reliable. That matters to me because I have spent a lot of time learning, changing direction, and working on projects that did not always reach completion.

Five years ago, I probably imagined success more simply: finding stable work, earning a better salary, and moving forward through a normal career path. I thought security would come mainly from having the right job.

Now I see it differently. A job can provide income, but it does not necessarily provide control or long-term security. Today, success looks more like owning useful skills, systems, and products that can continue creating value even if one opportunity disappears.

Five years ago, I wanted a safer position inside an existing path. Now I want to build a path of my own.

Ultimately, I want my work to mean that the pressure, failures, risks, and time I spent learning became something valuable — not only for me, but for my parents and the future I am trying to create.

---

**Q7: What do you know now that you wish someone had told you earlier — not a platitude, something specific and true that you had to learn the hard way?**

A: I wish someone had told me earlier that being under financial pressure makes you more vulnerable to bad opportunities, not better at recognising good ones.

When you urgently need your situation to change, anything with large potential upside can start to look more reasonable than it actually is. A trading strategy, an ambitious project, or a new technology can feel like the solution — not because the evidence is strong, but because you need it to work.

I had to learn that effort does not turn a weak process into a strong one. Spending months on a project does not make it valuable. Optimising a trading strategy does not make it robust. A profitable backtest does not mean it will make money live. Adding more features does not mean the product is closer to completion.

I also wish I had understood that constantly changing direction can feel like progress while quietly preventing it. Every new tool, model, feature, or project gives you fresh energy, but restarting repeatedly means you never stay with one difficult problem long enough to finish and validate it.

The lesson I learned the hard way is that you need rules before you are emotionally invested. Decide beforehand how much time or money you are willing to risk, what evidence would justify continuing, and what failure conditions mean you must stop. Once you are deep inside something, it becomes much harder to judge it honestly.

Now I know that the right question is not, "Could this change my life?" It is, "What evidence do I have, what can I afford to lose, and what would prove that I should stop?"

That would not have removed every failure, but it would have saved me time, money, and a lot of false confidence.

---

**Interviewer's note:** Across seven questions, one pattern repeats with unusual consistency: the gap between effort and evidence. Mohs has learned — the hard way — that working hard on something does not make it work, that optimising a weak process does not make it strong, and that urgency makes bad opportunities look necessary. What gives this more weight than ordinary self-awareness is that he can name the exact mechanism: financial pressure distorts judgment. The second pattern running underneath everything is his parents. They are not background detail — they are the engine behind nearly every decision he described, from the risk he chose to take to the reason he cannot afford to waste time. The tension in this transcript is between someone who understands the lessons clearly and someone still under enough pressure that those lessons will be genuinely tested.
